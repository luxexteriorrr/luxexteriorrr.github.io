This is a page about ambience, about places, and about how some of them disappear.

It is made from what I remember more than from what I have. A few walks, a list of dates, a camera pointed at a pond, some places online that are not there anymore. It is an interface less for navigation, more for presence.

Most of the web is built to reward your attention. The longer you look, the more it gives you: another post, another video, another page loading before you reach the end of this one. This page works the other way round. It gives you nothing extra for looking. It only stays while you are here.

When you stop moving, the words start to go, one by one, and the pond goes with them. Nothing is deleted. It is only left alone, and on the web that is how most things disappear. When you move again, it comes back.

So the reading is on you. Read slowly, but keep a hand on it.

Today we talk about ambience. A suburban upbringing does not come without a sense of privilege: the ability to take a moment away from the problems in urban living — not the ones the Velvet Underground sang about, but more the sort that are present in Metal Machine Music. The constant clattering, the noise — the hymns of the city.

Growing up, there was the simple pleasure of finding so-called peace within a singular presence. It is an escape. Closing a loop, the city expands, replacing the bark trees with concrete ones. But the problems in urban living are not what this piece is written for — today we talk about ambience.

Years later, when the desire for ambience has turned into a habit, its absence and presence become so apparent and obvious. You start looking for that same sense not just in its physical manifestations, but far beyond them. You learn to cherish that feeling; you start to see it in places where perhaps it is not to be seen.

And then there is the web — vast, silent, fragile. Once built as an eternal storage of information, now cluttered with noise. But between the static and the scroll, there are still shadows of something slower. Interfaces that breathe. A hyperlink, like a path. A webpage, like a memory. The walk doesn’t end at the screen — it refracts through it. This is a trace of that walk.

WALK 9 of June, 2020

I remember the first time I visited Hampstead Heath; it was during the pandemic. I was trying to grasp that ambience amongst the noise, something that resembled that calm, the stillness of a childhood nostalgia for simpler times. It was not a mediated or manufactured one, but the very essence of true calm — the natural rhythm of leaves, distant birdsong, gravel underfoot screeching to the beat of the steps.

It was almost like an interface opening up. There was something about rediscovering such a feeling for the first time. It felt somehow computational, in a strange yet so evident way. The feeling emerged through interaction — input, output, sensing, responding.

But poetic, not so much mechanical.

The memory of it, though faint, is somewhere there. The interface is somewhere there.

Somewhere now, this water ripples, light bending on the surface, the birds vanishing behind the vegetation.

You are there, watching. But the camera offers no warmth, no scent of the air, no sound of that gravel under your feet, no gentle smell of calm.

Illusion of access. The stream is a window, just like what you are reading. A transmission without a touch. One night a picture was taken of the gentle greens. You can touch it, feel it, sense it.

But you are not there. You are a passive observer: you cannot change anything, merely see what is.

Yet something about it still lingers, a digital echo of that walk. A soft reminder of where I once stood and where you might stand.

Ambience, once found in presence, now shimmers through this transmission. Unreachable, unresponsive, and somehow still affecting.

LOG IN

My grandad bought me my first laptop. I don't remember the make, only that it was black. It was in my room, in Moscow.

Before that, the web reached me through my sister. She downloaded music from Zaycev.net and put it on my mp3 player, and I listened to whatever she had picked.

Later there was VK. That was where I talked to my classmates, played games, wrote on their walls, and we gave each other gifts.

I still go back to them. Zaycev.net is blocked now, because of the rights to the music. VK asks me to log in, and to log in it sends a code to a phone number I don't have anymore, in a city I don't live in anymore.

The Heath I can walk back into. These I can't.

There was a time, not too long ago, when that same feeling — drifting through margins, the sensing, the quiet — could be found on the internet. Not in the structured, monetised version of it we scroll through today, but in the earlier, messier one. The one full of odd little corners, strange dead ends, and half-finished ideas just like the one in front of you.

It was a different kind of noise — softer, somehow. Fuzzier.

Back then, the web felt like a landscape. Not a product.

There were places where that ambience lived. The kind that didn’t shout, didn’t sell. Just sat there, waiting. Maybe a blog last updated in 2009. Maybe a zipped folder passed between friends.

But as we started moving away from all these things, those places started vanishing.

They did not all go the same way. Some broke: a link that returns 404, a file format no browser opens anymore. Some were blocked, for the rights or by the law. Some were sold, and closed behind a login, a subscription, a paywall. Some were just left, with nobody paying for them and nobody looking after them.

The ambience leaked out. The ghosts stayed behind.
